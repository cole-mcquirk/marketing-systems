# Search & Content Planning

Search data is one input into content planning.

It becomes more useful when combined with customer questions, sales feedback, subject-matter expertise, business priorities, existing performance, and the resources available to create something properly.

The aim is to find topics where audience interest, organizational relevance, and useful expertise overlap.

---

## Where ideas come from

Content opportunities can come from a lot of places:

* Customer interviews
* Lead inquiries
* Sales conversations
* Subject-matter experts
* Search research
* Existing organic performance
* Product and business priorities
* Competitor content
* Industry publications
* Questions appearing repeatedly at events
* Gaps in the current website
* New technologies or changes in the market
* Content that already performs well and could be expanded

These inputs become more useful when they are considered together.

A topic appearing in search data becomes more interesting when Sales is hearing the same question.

A business priority becomes easier to support when there is already evidence that people are looking for information around it.

An SME conversation may uncover an opportunity that keyword tools would never have suggested.

---

## Start with the audience

Before looking too closely at volume, establish who the content is actually for.

Useful questions include:

* Which audience are we trying to reach?
* What are they trying to understand?
* Is this connected to a problem they actually have?
* How close is the topic to something the organization can help with?
* Is the person searching likely to be a buyer, an influencer, a practitioner, or a broader audience?
* What should they understand or do after engaging with the content?

The same keyword can have very different value depending on who is searching and why.

---

## Understand the search behavior

Once the audience and topic make sense, search research can show how people are approaching the subject.

Useful inputs include:

* Search terms
* Questions
* Search volume
* Intent
* Related searches
* Current rankings
* Competitor visibility
* SERP results
* Existing pages on the organization's own site
* Changes in search behavior over time
* AI and LLM visibility where relevant

The wording of searches can reveal:

* How familiar the audience is with the subject
* Which terminology they use
* Where there is confusion
* Whether they want education, comparison, troubleshooting, or a provider
* How technical the eventual content needs to be

---

## Search is one signal among several

Content ideas should be evaluated against a wider set of inputs.

### Search & content planning inputs

| Input                   | What to look for                                                                    |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Audience need**       | Does this answer something the audience actually cares about?                       |
| **Search behavior**     | Are people looking for it, and what language are they using?                        |
| **Business relevance**  | Does it support something the organization wants to grow, explain, or strengthen?   |
| **Existing visibility** | Are there already rankings, traffic, authority, or useful content around the topic? |
| **Content gap**         | Is there room to add something genuinely useful?                                    |
| **Internal expertise**  | Are there people who can add real knowledge or experience?                          |
| **Resources**           | Can the content be produced properly?                                               |
| **Validation needs**    | What needs checking before publication?                                             |
| **Reuse potential**     | Can the work support other channels or teams as well?                               |

The strongest opportunities usually show value across several of these signals.

---

## Business relevance changes the value of a topic

A topic with modest search volume can still be extremely useful, especially in technical B2B markets.

A few hundred highly relevant searches from the right type of organization may be worth more than thousands of visits from a broad audience with little connection to the business.

Large search volumes also come with larger questions:

* How relevant is the audience?
* How difficult is the topic to compete for?
* What happens after someone lands on the page?
* Is there a realistic next step?
* Does the organization have anything useful to contribute?

Search volume provides context. It does not set the priority on its own.

---

## Look at what already exists

Review the current search results before deciding what to create.

Look for:

* Who currently ranks
* What kind of content dominates
* How technical the existing material is
* Whether the results match the intended audience
* What questions are already answered well
* What is missing
* Whether the existing material is outdated
* Whether another format or perspective would be more useful

Sometimes there is an obvious gap.

Sometimes the existing results already answer the question extremely well.

Both are useful findings.

---

## Find a reason to create it

Before creating content, answer one question clearly:

> What are we adding?

That could be:

* A clearer explanation
* More technical depth
* A simpler explanation of something highly technical
* Original research
* First-hand experience
* SME knowledge
* A useful diagram
* A comparison
* A practical process
* A tool
* A dataset
* A different audience perspective
* A better answer to a recurring customer question

If the only justification is the existence of a keyword, the idea probably needs more work.

---

## Use internal expertise

Some of the strongest content opportunities already exist inside the organization.

Sales may know the questions that appear in almost every customer conversation.

Technical teams may know which concepts are consistently misunderstood.

Product teams may know where existing explanations leave out important details.

Training or education teams may already have material that can be adapted for a broader audience.

The opportunity is to identify which parts of that knowledge are useful externally and turn them into something people can find and understand.

---

## Build useful questions for SMEs

When technical knowledge is missing, define the gaps before the SME conversation.

For example:

* What does this mean in practical terms?
* Which part tends to confuse customers?
* What claim can be made confidently?
* What evidence supports it?
* Is there an important trade-off to explain?
* Which terminology would an expert use?
* Which terminology would the audience use?
* Is there anything here that should stay private?
* Could this be explained with an example, diagram, or comparison?

Specific questions usually produce much better material than asking someone to "explain the topic."

---

## Validation matters

Technical content usually needs another set of eyes before publication.

The exact process depends on the organization, but there should be clarity around:

* Who owns technical accuracy
* Which claims need approval
* Which data can be published
* Which terminology has to be precise
* Which information is commercially sensitive
* Which information may involve IP
* Who gives final approval

The process should protect accuracy without creating an endless review cycle.

---

## Decide what stays private

Useful content benefits from specificity, but some information belongs inside the organization.

A simple three-way split helps:

### Safe to publish

Useful knowledge the organization is comfortable making public.

### Needs validation

Technical explanations, claims, data, comparisons, or other details that require review.

### Keep internal

Customer information, sensitive commercial information, proprietary processes, protected IP, credentials, internal data, and anything else that should remain private.

Making this distinction early avoids problems later.

---

## Broad topics can build the foundation

Some content has a broader educational purpose.

It can help:

* Build organic visibility
* Strengthen topical authority
* Reach new audiences
* Earn links
* Introduce the organization earlier in the journey
* Generate more information about what audiences care about

As site authority grows, more specific and commercially relevant topics may become easier to compete for.

A simplified progression:

**Broad useful content → Organic visibility → More authority → More targeted opportunities → Higher-intent content**

Broad content still needs a reason to exist. Traffic alone is a weak objective.

---

## Strategic topics may have very little search volume

Keyword tools reflect established search behavior.

Organizations also need to communicate around:

* New categories
* New technologies
* Research
* Emerging problems
* Original ideas
* Topics the market has not developed consistent language around yet

Search volume may be close to zero.

Those topics may be better suited to:

* PR
* Trade publications
* Social
* Email
* Events
* Thought leadership
* Sales conversations
* Educational resources
* Research
* Partnerships

SEO does not need to be the justification for every useful idea.

## Plan for reuse

Content becomes more valuable when it can travel across channels and teams.

A useful technical resource might eventually support:

* Organic search
* Paid search
* Social
* Email
* Sales
* PR
* Events
* Training
* Video
* Customer education

Thinking about reuse during planning can influence how the original asset is researched, structured, designed, and validated.

---

## AI and LLM discovery

AI assistants are becoming another part of the discovery environment.

Useful questions include:

* Does the organization appear for relevant questions?
* Is it described accurately?
* Which sources appear to influence the answers?
* Is authoritative information missing?
* Are important concepts clearly explained on the organization's own site?
* Could primary-source information be made easier to find and understand?

This area is changing quickly, so it makes more sense to treat AI visibility as an evolving discovery channel than as a finished playbook.

The current foundation is straightforward: publish useful, accurate, well-structured information and watch how different discovery systems use it.

## A practical working template

When evaluating a content opportunity, try to get a reasonable answer to questions like these:

| Question                                       | Notes / Inputs |
| ---------------------------------------------- | -------------- |
| Who is this for?                               |                |
| What are they trying to understand or solve?   |                |
| How do we know this matters to them?           |                |
| What are people searching for?                 |                |
| What language are they using?                  |                |
| What does the search intent look like?         |                |
| What already ranks?                            |                |
| What already exists on the site?               |                |
| Is there a clear gap in the existing content?  |                |
| What could we add that is actually useful?     |                |
| Why does this matter to the organization?      |                |
| Is there internal expertise on the subject?    |                |
| Who should be involved in developing it?       |                |
| What needs technical validation?               |                |
| Is anything sensitive or proprietary?          |                |
| What format makes the most sense?              |                |
| Where else could the work be reused?           |                |
| What should someone do after engaging with it? |                |
| How will it be measured?                       |                |

There will usually be blanks at the beginning.

Those gaps show where more research or conversations are needed before committing resources.

---

## A few things worth keeping in mind

### Search volume needs context

A small number can represent a highly relevant audience. A large number can represent almost nobody the organization needs to reach.

### Look at the actual results

The SERP usually gives more context than the volume column alone.

### Internal expertise can be a competitive advantage

Organizations often have valuable knowledge that has never been translated into searchable, understandable content.

### Content planning works better across teams

Sales, technical teams, product, education, agencies, and Marketing all see different parts of the same market.

Combining those perspectives usually produces better ideas.

### Some of the best topics are already hiding in plain sight

Lead inquiries, customer questions, technical presentations, training material, sales conversations, and existing resources are often full of potential topics.

The job is finding the useful ones and figuring out what to do with them.
